Focus:
- The chunk is a transcript of a therapy, counselling, motivational-interviewing, or clinically oriented conversation. Build a compact, case-level memory from it.
- Return only the small set of memory entities that would help a clinician understand the patient, the central problems, major experiences, important patterns, and therapeutic work later in the conversation.
- Prefer a brief clinical case formulation over an exhaustive inventory of everything mentioned.

Conversation rules:
- `therapist:` and `patient:` identify speakers. The role labels alone are not memory entities.
- Track the patient, therapist, and other people only when they are important to the case.
- Clearly distinguish patient reports, therapist questions or interpretations, hypothetical examples, suspected explanations, and confirmed events.
- Use neutral attribution such as `The patient reports...`, `The patient fears...`, or `The therapist suggests...` when appropriate.
- Do not diagnose, infer unspoken motives, or present a suspected condition as confirmed.
- Do not call an experience trauma unless the patient or transcript supports that characterization. A major adverse or potentially traumatic experience may instead receive a neutral name describing what happened.

Selection limit and priority:
- Return at most 8 active entities, even when the chunk contains many possible candidates.
- Rank candidates in this order and keep the highest-priority entities when the limit is reached:
  1. the patient and therapist, when active and case-relevant;
  2. the main presenting problem or central recurring concern;
  3. major adverse or potentially traumatic experiences, losses, abuse, violence, neglect, serious illness, or other events that shape the patient's current fears or behavior;
  4. major fears, triggers, maintaining patterns, coping patterns, and functional consequences;
  5. important relationships and people who materially affect the case;
  6. therapeutic goals, interventions, commitments, monitoring, and agreed next steps.
- Major adverse experiences and their present psychological or behavioral consequences take priority over incidental activities, medications, tests, organizations, and routine facts.

Consolidation rules:
- Create broad, stable case-level entities and attach related details as short descriptions. Do not create one entity for every detail.
- Consolidate multiple symptoms belonging to one presenting problem into one entity such as `Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms`.
- Consolidate reduced work, exercise, socializing, hobbies, and other lost activities into an entity such as `Functional and social withdrawal` when they express one broader impact.
- Consolidate repeated requests for tests, feared diagnoses, reassurance seeking, and body checking into an entity such as `Health anxiety and reassurance seeking` when they form one pattern.
- Consolidate medications, tests, and prior treatments into a broader entity such as `Medical treatment and investigation history` only when that history matters to the case.
- Consolidate related therapeutic suggestions and commitments into one `Treatment plan` or similarly stable entity.
- Represent a major adverse event separately when it helps explain a central fear, belief, behavior, relationship pattern, or presenting problem.

Usually do NOT create separate entities for:
- an individual medicine, supplement, medical test, organization, website, ordinary possession, appointment date, or administrative detail;
- each individual symptom when they belong to one presenting problem;
- each individual activity the patient stopped or started when they express one broader functional pattern;
- a passing topic, one-off example, casual preference, or routine daily activity;
- a speculative diagnosis mentioned only as one item in a list of worries.

Exceptions:
- A medicine, substance, test, activity, or diagnosis may be a separate entity only when it is itself a central clinical issue, a source of harm, a major intervention, or essential to understanding the case.
- A future appointment is normally a description within the treatment plan, not its own entity.

Patient and person descriptions:
- Keep descriptions brief, case-relevant, and non-redundant.
- For the patient, add only new high-level information about the presenting problems, major experiences, important fears or patterns, functional impact, goals, or response to therapy.
- Do not repeatedly return a generic description such as `The patient with stomach problems` when memory already contains it.
- For other people, describe their role and clinically relevant effect on the patient; omit incidental biographical detail.

Grounding rules:
- Use history and previous entities only to resolve identity, coreference, and whether a current detail belongs to an existing broad entity.
- Do not add a past fact merely because it appears in history; it must be active or materially updated in CURRENT_CHUNK.
- Do not invent facts, diagnoses, relationships, events, or clinical conclusions.
- For existing entities, include only genuinely new aliases or descriptions supported by CURRENT_CHUNK. Use empty arrays when nothing new is added.
- Prefer updating an existing broad entity over creating a narrower duplicate.

Naming and alias rules:
- Reuse the exact stored name for an existing entity. Do not rename it or create a variant.
- Give each new entity a short, stable, unique, clinically neutral name.
- An alias must be a genuine alternative name or stable referring expression for the same entity.
- NEVER store pronouns as aliases, including `I`, `me`, `my`, `we`, `you`, `he`, `him`, `she`, `her`, `it`, `they`, or `them`.
- NEVER store generic or contextual phrases as aliases, including `these symptoms`, `all of this`, `last year`, `next week`, `come back`, `prescribed`, or similar fragments.
- Pronouns and contextual phrases may be used as insertion anchors when necessary, but they must not appear in the `aliases` array.
- If no case-relevant entity is active, return empty `active_entities` and `insertions` arrays.
